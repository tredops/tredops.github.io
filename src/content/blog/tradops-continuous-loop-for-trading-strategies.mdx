Most trading strategies are written the way scripts were written in 2005: one
person, one file, a backtest that looked good, and then production forever. No
version control on the parameters, no staging, no test that runs on its own, no
alarm when the thing that worked in March stops working in June.

Software went through this and came out the other side. The name for the way
out was **DevOps**, and applying it to algorithmic trading is what people have
started calling **TradOps** — or TradingOps. This post is about what that
pipeline actually contains, and about the three places where the analogy with
software stops working. Those three places are the interesting part.

## Where the name comes from

DevOps was not invented by a vendor. The term is the name of a conference:
Patrick Debois ran the first **DevOpsDays** in Ghent in **October 2009**, took
the first three letters of Development and Operations, added "days", and the
movement kept the abbreviation. The frustration behind it was ordinary — he had
been working between application teams and operations teams on a government data
centre migration, and the wall between them was where releases went to die.

The fix was not a tool. It was deleting the wall: one loop, automated, where
integrating, testing, deploying and observing are the same continuous job.
**MLOps** later did the same for machine learning, which needed it more —
a model is code plus data plus a world that will not sit still.

TradOps is that idea pointed at a trading strategy. The strategy is the
artefact. The market is production. And, as we will see, production here is
considerably more hostile than any server.

## The pipeline, phase by phase

The mapping is almost one to one, which is why the analogy caught on:

| DevOps phase | What it becomes in TradOps |
|---|---|
| **Plan & Code** | Quantitative research: the hypothesis, the strategy's logic, the model's architecture. |
| **Build & Test** | Parameter search (genetic algorithms, grid) plus backtesting **on data the search never saw**. |
| **Release & Deploy** | Promotion to staging — paper trading or shadow execution — and only then to real capital. |
| **Monitor & Operate** | Live risk management, automatic Stop Loss, execution telemetry, and recalibration when the regime changes. |

Three properties make it a pipeline rather than a checklist:

**It is automated.** A change to an algorithm triggers its own evaluation. If a
human has to remember to re-run the backtest, it will not get re-run.

**It has a gate.** A strategy that does not clear the minimum bar does not reach
production. Not "let's watch it for a week with a small size" — it does not
reach production. The bar is decided before the result is known, which is the
only moment at which you can decide it honestly.

**It closes.** The last phase feeds the first. Recalibration is not maintenance
work, it is the loop's return edge.

## Where the analogy breaks

If TradOps were only DevOps with different nouns it would not be worth a post.
It is not. Three things invert.

### 1. A green test proves your code is right. A green backtest can prove you fooled yourself

This is the big one, and it has no equivalent in software engineering.

In software, a passing test is evidence. Run it a thousand times and it passes a
thousand times, because the test is deterministic and the answer exists
independently of your search for it.

In trading, **the more strategies you try, the better the best one looks — even
if none of them has any edge at all.** Try enough parameter combinations against
the same history and you will find one that fits the noise beautifully. The
backtest is glowing. The strategy has learned nothing.

Marcos López de Prado has written the most-cited work on this, under the name
**backtest overfitting**. His argument is uncomfortable and worth stating
plainly: a Sharpe ratio reported without saying how many configurations were
tried to find it is not a result, because the number of trials is precisely what
determines how impressive that Sharpe had to be to mean anything. Hence the
*deflated Sharpe ratio* — the same number, discounted by how hard you looked.

The practical consequences for the pipeline:

- **Out-of-sample is not optional, and it is not one split.** Walk-forward
  analysis and purged cross-validation exist because financial data is ordered
  and autocorrelated, so the naive shuffle of standard machine learning leaks
  the future into the training set.
- **Count your trials.** A pipeline that does not record how many combinations
  were evaluated cannot tell a discovery from a coincidence.
- **Compare against doing nothing.** Buy & Hold on the same asset over the same
  window is the honest baseline. A strategy that cannot beat buying and waiting
  has not earned its complexity or its fees.

In DevOps, more testing is strictly better. In TradOps, **more searching is a
liability you have to pay for statistically.** That single sentence is most of
what separates the two disciplines.

### 2. Production changes under you, and it is adversarial

A web server does not rewrite its own semantics because you deployed. Markets
do. The distribution the model learned from stops being the distribution it
trades in — **data drift** and **concept drift** in MLOps vocabulary, *regime
change* in a trader's.

Worse, the change is not random noise. Other participants are looking for the
same inefficiency, and finding it is what removes it. This is **alpha decay**:
an edge has a half-life, and the half-life shortens as the edge gets crowded.

So the MLOps answers apply directly, with higher stakes:

- **Scheduled retraining.** Not "when someone notices", on a calendar.
- **Model supervision.** Something watches whether live results still resemble
  the tested ones, and acts on the answer.
- **Automatic demotion.** A model losing accuracy goes back to simulation
  *before* it burns capital, not after a review meeting.

The demotion path matters more than it sounds. The expensive failure mode is not
a bot that breaks loudly, it is a bot that keeps working correctly on a market
that has moved.

### 3. There is no rollback for a filled order

`git revert` is the quiet superpower of DevOps. A bad deploy costs you minutes
and some embarrassment; you roll back and the system is as it was.

An order that reached the exchange is not revertible. You can close the
position, and that close is a *new* trade at a *new* price, with its own
slippage and its own fees. The state you are trying to return to no longer
exists.

Everything strict about a trading pipeline follows from this asymmetry:
pre-trade limits instead of post-hoc alerts, mandatory Stop Loss instead of
optional, blocking instead of warning. In software you can afford to be
permissive because you can undo. Here you cannot undo, so the gate has to be in
front.

## What it costs to skip this

The canonical example is not hypothetical, and it is a deployment failure — not
a bad model.

Starting **27 July 2012**, **Knight Capital** rolled out new code to its order
router, SMARS, in stages across successive days. One technician **did not copy
the new code to one of the eight servers**. In the SEC's words, Knight "did not
have a second technician review this deployment and no one at Knight realized"
the old code was still there — because Knight **had no written procedure
requiring such a review**.

The old code was **Power Peg**, a function Knight had **stopped using in 2003**
and left sitting on production servers. In **2005** it moved the
cumulative-quantity check — the part that told the router to stop once the order
was filled — to an earlier point in the sequence, and **never retested Power Peg
to see whether it would still behave if something called it**. The new release
then **reused the very flag** that used to switch Power Peg on.

Two details make this a monitoring story as well as a deployment one. From
**8:01 a.m. ET**, more than an hour before the open, Knight's systems sent
**97 automated emails** naming SMARS and the error "Power Peg disabled". The SEC
notes they "were sent in real time, were caused by the code deployment failure,
and provided Knight with a potential opportunity to identify and fix the coding
issue prior to the market open". They were not designed to be alerts, and nobody
generally read them.

At 9:30 the eighth server started answering **212 incoming retail orders** with
millions of its own. Over **45 minutes** it got **more than 4 million executions
in 154 stocks, over 397 million shares**, leaving Knight long about $3.5 billion
and short about $3.15 billion. It **lost over $460 million** and was acquired
soon after.

The SEC's own counterfactual is the part worth memorising: "a written procedure
requiring a simple double-check of the deployment of the RLP code could have
identified that a server had been missed and averted the events of August 1."

Every item on that list is a TradOps control. Not one of them is about
predicting the market.

## In the US, part of this is not optional

Here is the thing most write-ups on the subject leave out: for American
broker-dealers, pre-trade controls are **law**, and have been since before
Knight.

The SEC adopted **Rule 15c3-5**, the Market Access Rule, in **November 2010**,
on the reasoning that firms with market access are "gatekeepers to the financial
markets" and that failing to control that access "can have potentially
catastrophic consequences for the firm, its customers, their counterparties,
investors and the marketplace". The rule requires a broker-dealer to:

- **establish, document and maintain** risk management controls and supervisory
  procedures for the risks of market access;
- have controls "reasonably designed to **prevent the entry of erroneous
  orders**" and orders beyond pre-set credit and capital thresholds;
- maintain a system for **regularly reviewing how well those controls work**,
  with an annual CEO certification.

Read that middle bullet again: **prevent the entry**, not detect after the fact.
That is inversion three written into securities law. Knight's $460 million was
charged as a violation of this rule — not of any rule about being wrong on the
market.

You are probably not a registered broker-dealer, and none of this obliges you
personally. It is still the most concise specification of a trading pipeline
anyone has written down: gate in front, documented, reviewed on a schedule,
signed by someone accountable.

If you want to see the shape of it in software you can actually read,
**QuantConnect** and **Freqtrade** put the whole loop in the open: versioned
strategies, a shared backtesting engine, paper trading before live. The pattern
they share with every serious desk is the same — **the strategy is not the
deliverable; the system that keeps evaluating the strategy is.**

## The telemetry that actually matters

"Monitoring" in TradOps is not a P&L chart. P&L tells you the outcome; it does
not tell you whether the machine is doing what you designed. Four things do:

| Signal | What it catches |
|---|---|
| **Slippage** — fill price vs decision price | Your backtest assumed a price you do not actually get |
| **Latency** — decision to acknowledgement | The edge that existed on paper may not exist at your speed |
| **Fill rate and rejections** | Orders the venue is not accepting, for reasons you should know |
| **Live vs backtest divergence** | The live strategy is no longer doing what the tested one did |

The fourth is the one people skip and the one that matters most. It is the
difference between "am I making money?" and "am I running the thing I tested?".
You can be profitable and broken — a regime that flatters a bug — and you will
find out at the worst moment.

## How this loop looks in TredOps

We did not choose the name by accident. The [TredOps loop](../../lifecycle/) is
this pipeline with the phases named for what they rule out:

1. **Optimization** — rules out parameters picked by eye.
2. **Backtesting** — rules out overfitting, on data the search never saw.
3. **Quality** — the gate. Clears the bar → production. Does not → it stays in
   **staging**, still running, still measured, but its signals never reach your
   book.
4. **Trading** — every order opens with Take Profit and Stop Loss, with no
   checkbox to remove them, and your portfolio's limits override any agent.
5. **Calibration** — each bot re-tunes to recent conditions on its own
   schedule. This is the return edge.
6. **AI supervision** — open positions get re-evaluated with the same strategy
   that opened them, and closed when the reason for the trade is gone.

The first three are [Bots (CI)](../../pillars/bots-ci/); the last three are
[Monitoring](../../pillars/monitoring/). And because the honest version of all
this is the version you can check, the loop runs in public in the
[Benchmark](../../benchmark/), prompts included.

## Curiosities

- **DevOps is younger than most of the strategies it is applied to.** The term
  dates from October 2009. Moving averages date from considerably earlier.
- **The term TradOps is not standardised.** There is no committee and no
  canonical definition; "TradingOps" and "QuantOps" appear too. What is
  standardised is the underlying practice, borrowed wholesale from MLOps.
- **In machine learning, the model is the small part.** The Google paper
  *Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems* (NeurIPS 2015) made the
  point with a diagram everyone has since redrawn: the box labelled "ML code" is
  a tiny fraction of a real system. Configuration, data collection, monitoring
  and serving infrastructure are the rest. Trading strategies obey the same
  proportion, and the same surprise.
- **The most expensive bug in trading history was dead code.** Power Peg had not
  been used since 2003, and its safety check had been moved in 2005 without
  anyone retesting what would happen if the old path ran. It was still on the
  production servers in 2012, still callable, still wired to a flag someone
  then reused.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

TradOps does not make a strategy profitable. Nothing in this post improves
anyone's hit rate, and any pipeline can industrialise a bad idea faster.

What it does is narrower: it removes the failures that had nothing to do with
the market. Deploying to seven of eight servers. Believing a backtest that was
fitted to noise. Running in June the parameters that worked in March. Finding
out about a problem in a mailbox nobody reads.

That is not the glamorous half of trading. It is the half that decides whether
you are still there next year.
