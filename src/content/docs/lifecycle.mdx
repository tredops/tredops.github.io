TredOps takes its name from **DevOps**, and not as a pun: the idea is the same.
In DevOps, software is not written once and abandoned — it enters a loop of
integration, testing, deployment and observation that never stops. Here, what
travels that loop is a **trading strategy**.

<LifecycleLoop lang="en" />

The left loop is **Trading**: what happens before a strategy touches real money.
The right one is **Ops**: what happens while it trades. Neither ever ends. A
strategy that clears every check today has to clear them again in two weeks,
because the market two weeks from now is not this one.

## Why each check exists

Every stop on the route is there to rule out one specific failure mode. Here
they are, in the order they are crossed.

### 1. Optimization

**Rules out:** parameters picked by eye.

A strategy's parameters (how far back it looks, how much deviation it tolerates,
where it sets its target) are not chosen by hand. They are searched for with
**genetic algorithms** over the asset's real history: combinations are scored,
the best ones crossed and mutated, across generations.

This guarantees nothing on its own — it is in fact the step that most easily
fools you — which is why it is never the last one.

### 2. Backtesting

**Rules out:** overfitting.

An optimised strategy always looks good on the data it was optimised against.
The question that matters is a different one: **what does it do with data it
never saw?**

Backtesting runs it over periods left out of the optimisation, and puts it up
against what was not planned for: opening gaps, volatility spikes, regime
changes. The result is always compared to **Buy & Hold** on the same asset,
because a strategy that cannot beat buying and waiting is not worth its
complexity.

:::note[What makes a backtest credible]
Every evaluation uses **only data from before the moment being evaluated**. This
is what avoiding *look-ahead bias* means: deciding with information that, at the
time, did not exist. A backtest that skips this produces spectacular and
completely useless results.
:::

### 3. Quality

**Rules out:** anything below the bar.

There is an automatic threshold here, and it is binary:

- **Clears it** → the bot goes to **production** and its signals enter the
  Signal Pool.
- **Does not** → it stays in **staging**: still running in the background,
  still accumulating results, but its signals never reach your book.

A bot in staging is not rejected; it is unproven. Nothing reaches your money via
"well, let's see how it goes".

### 4. Trading

**Rules out:** trades without a net.

This is where the strategy actually trades, and where two rules kick in that
cannot be switched off:

- **Every order opens with a Take Profit and a Stop Loss.** There is no checkbox
  to remove it — not for agents, not for scheduled tasks, not in autonomous
  mode.
- **Your portfolio's rules override any agent.** If a decision exceeds your
  maximum per position or eats into your cash reserve, the trade does not open.
  It is a block, not a warning.

On top of that, a bot's signal is **never** executed on its own: it goes through
the Signal Pool, through your portfolio's filters and through an agent's
decision. Three layers between "the bot saw something" and "you have an open
position".

### 5. Calibration

**Rules out:** stale parameters.

A parameter that worked in a low-volatility regime stops working in a high one.
So every bot has its own **calibration schedule** and periodically re-tunes its
parameters to recent conditions, without you doing anything.

This is the check that makes it a loop rather than a straight line: calibration
sends the strategy back to the start.

### 6. AI supervision

**Rules out:** forgotten positions.

While a position is open, a periodic process reviews it: it pulls the current
price, **re-evaluates the position with the same strategy that opened it**, and
closes it on Take Profit, on Stop Loss, or when the strategy itself says to get
out before either.

That last condition is the important one: you do not wait for a price level if
the reason for being in the trade has already gone.

Above that, your [agents](../guides/first-agent/) and
[scheduled tasks](../guides/agent-tasks/) add the layer of judgement: reviewing
sector concentration, cutting exposure, closing what has been going nowhere for
too long.

## What this loop does not do

Worth saying just as plainly:

- **It does not predict the market.** It rules out avoidable failures; it does
  not get things right more often.
- **It does not remove risk.** A Stop Loss bounds a loss, it does not prevent
  one.
- **It does not replace your judgement.** You set the limits at each layer, and
  the limits you set are the ones that apply.

The goal is more modest and more useful: that no loss comes from not having
decided in advance when to get out.
